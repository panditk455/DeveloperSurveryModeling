SOME WRITEUP

DATA AND METHODS

We analyzed data from the 2023 Stack Overflow Developer Survey, starting with
10,259 respondents. We selected nine variables of interest: job satisfaction,
AI tool adoption, years of professional experience, annual compensation,
education level, employment type, remote work status, and country. Job
satisfaction (originally rated 0-10) was recoded into three ordered categories,
Low (0-5), Moderate (6-7), and High (8-10), making it suitable for an ordinal
regression framework. Annual compensation was right-skewed with values reaching
$2M, so we applied a log transformation before centering at its mean (log-scale
mean of about 10.87). Years of professional experience was also centered at its
mean of approximately 11 years so that model intercepts and threshold estimates
reflect a developer at average experience rather than at zero years. Two text
values ("Less than 1 year" and "More than 50 years") were recoded to 0.5 and 50
before numeric conversion. Education was collapsed from many detailed categories
into four groups: No Degree, Bachelor's, Master's, and Professional/PhD.
Employment was similarly collapsed into Full-time, Freelance, Part-time,
Student, and Not employed. After applying a complete case analysis across all
selected variables, we retained 4,129 respondents out of 10,253 (a 59.7%
reduction, driven largely by missing compensation data).

Because respondents were clustered within 125 countries, we fit a Cumulative
Link Mixed Model (CLMM) using the ordinal package in R. The CLMM extends the
proportional odds logistic regression model by adding a country-level random
intercept, which allows the baseline log-odds of satisfaction to shift up or
down for each country. The model assumes that for any predictor x, the
cumulative log-odds of being in category j or below follows the form:
logit(P(Y <= j | x)) = threshold_j - (beta * x + u_country), where u_country
is the country random effect drawn from a normal distribution with mean zero
and estimated variance. This parameterization means a positive beta coefficient
corresponds to higher predicted satisfaction (higher odds of being in a higher
category). The model was fitted by Laplace approximation.


EXPLORATORY DATA ANALYSIS

Among the 4,129 respondents in the analytic sample, about half (48.2%) reported
high job satisfaction, roughly a third (32.3%) reported moderate satisfaction,
and just under one in five (19.5%) reported low satisfaction, so the response
is noticeably skewed toward higher satisfaction. To check whether the
proportional odds assumption was plausible before modeling, we computed
cumulative log odds empirically for each predictor group. Specifically, we
computed logit(P(Y = Low)) and logit(P(Y <= Moderate)) for each level of the
categorical predictors. Under proportional odds, those two lines should be
approximately parallel (shifted vertically by the same constant across all
categories). The plots showed roughly parallel lines across AI adoption groups,
education levels, and remote work categories, which supports this assumption.
For the continuous predictors (centered experience and log compensation), we
grouped respondents into five quantile bins and plotted cumulative log odds
against the bin mean. Both showed decreasing log odds as the predictor
increased, and the two lines remained nearly parallel, consistent with linearity
and proportional odds on the log-odds scale.

A bar chart of the proportion of respondents reporting high satisfaction,
grouped by country (top 20 countries by sample size), showed modest variation
across countries, with rates generally ranging from about 40% to 60%. Canada,
Israel, and Switzerland appeared near the top while India was toward the lower
end. However, fitting a null CLMM with only a country random intercept and no
predictors gave an estimated country-level variance of 0.009, corresponding to
an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of approximately 0.003. This means
only 0.3% of the total variation in job satisfaction is attributable to
country-level differences, indicating that respondents within the same country
are barely more similar to each other than respondents from different countries.
This finding suggested that while a random effect for country is technically
justified (it keeps the standard errors honest), its practical contribution to
explaining satisfaction is very small.


MODELING: RANDOM EFFECT AND PROPORTIONAL ODDS

Before fitting the full model, we tested two key assumptions. First, we assessed
whether the country random intercept was needed. We fit a fixed-effects-only
cumulative link model (CLM, no random effect) with the same predictors and
compared it to the CLMM using a likelihood ratio test. Because a random effect
variance is constrained to be non-negative, the null value (variance = 0) sits
on the boundary of the parameter space, which means the standard chi-square
p-value is anti-conservative. We applied the standard boundary correction:
multiplying the one-sided chi-square p-value by 0.5 and adding 0.5 times the
probability at zero. The observed LRT statistic was 3.06, giving a
boundary-corrected p-value of 0.040. This is marginal but provides enough
evidence to retain the random effect, and doing so ensures that the standard
errors of the fixed effects correctly account for country-level clustering.
Second, we tested the proportional odds assumption for AI tool adoption,
which was our primary predictor of interest, using the VGAM package. We fit a
partial proportional odds model that allowed the AI adoption effect to differ
across the two cumulative thresholds (Low vs. Moderate+High, and Low+Moderate
vs. High), then compared it to the standard proportional odds model via a
likelihood ratio test. The test statistic was 2.45 on 2 degrees of freedom
(p = 0.29), and the partial model had a higher AIC (8502.7 vs. 8501.1),
providing no evidence against the proportional odds assumption for AI adoption.
We therefore proceeded with the full proportional odds specification.


MODEL SELECTION 

Starting from the main effects CLMM (Model 1), we considered two interaction
models: AI adoption interacted with years of experience (Model 2) and AI
adoption interacted with education level (Model 3). The rationale was that the
benefit of using AI tools might be stronger for less experienced developers
(who rely on AI more as a productivity aid) or might vary by education. Neither
interaction improved model fit. The AI-by-experience interaction produced an
LRT statistic of 2.23 on 2 degrees of freedom (p = 0.33, delta AIC = +1.8),
and the AI-by-education interaction produced an LRT of 4.94 on 6 degrees of
freedom (p = 0.55, delta AIC = +7.1). Model 1 had the lowest AIC (8500.05)
across all four models including the null (AIC = 8554.93), confirming it as the
best-fitting and most parsimonious specification.

In the final model (Model 1), four predictors were statistically significant.
Using AI tools was associated with a 0.20 increase in the cumulative log-odds
of being in a higher satisfaction category compared to not using AI (p = 0.004,
95% CI: 0.064 to 0.346), holding all other predictors constant. Planning to use
AI but not yet doing so showed no significant difference from non-users
(beta = -0.046, p = 0.64), suggesting the benefit is tied to actual use rather
than intention. Each additional centered year of professional experience was
associated with a 0.018 increase in cumulative log-odds of higher satisfaction
(p < 0.001, 95% CI: 0.011 to 0.026). A one-unit increase in centered log annual
compensation was associated with a 0.094 increase in cumulative log-odds
(p = 0.0008, 95% CI: 0.039 to 0.149). Working fully remotely was associated
with a 0.25 higher cumulative log-odds of satisfaction compared to working
in-person (p = 0.006, 95% CI: 0.070 to 0.421), while hybrid work showed no
significant difference from in-person (beta = 0.136, p = 0.12). Education level
and employment type were not significant after adjusting for the other
predictors. The estimated country random effect variance was 0.011
(SD = 0.104), confirming it remained small but nonzero after adding the fixed
effects. The two estimated threshold parameters were: Low|Moderate = -1.23 and
Moderate|High = 0.28, meaning that at average values of all predictors, the
log-odds of being Low versus Moderate or High is -1.23 (about 22% probability
of Low), and the log-odds of being Low or Moderate versus High is 0.28 (about
57% probability of High or below, so roughly 43% probability of High).


DIAGNOSTICS 

To assess model fit, we computed surrogate residuals using the sure package
applied to the equivalent fixed-effects proportional odds model (MASS::polr),
which has the same coefficient structure as the CLMM but without the random
intercept. Surrogate residuals extend the concept of continuous residuals to
ordinal models by replacing each observed category with a random draw from the
corresponding interval of the latent continuous distribution implied by the
model. Under a correctly specified model, these residuals should follow a
standard normal distribution and show no systematic pattern against the
predictors. The normal Q-Q plot followed the reference line closely through the
center of the distribution, with mild deviations at both tails, which is
typical behavior for ordinal models with only three categories and limited
probability mass in the tails. Plots of surrogate residuals against centered
years of experience and centered log compensation showed horizontal scatter
centered at zero with no curvature, supporting the linearity assumption for
both continuous predictors.

Overall, the analysis supports four clear conclusions. First, developers who
actively use AI tools report higher job satisfaction than those who do not,
and this effect is not moderated by experience or education level. Second,
satisfaction increases with both experience and compensation, consistent with
the idea that career progress and financial reward independently contribute to
wellbeing at work. Third, remote work is associated with higher satisfaction
relative to in-person work, with no statistically distinguishable difference
for hybrid arrangements. Fourth, country of residence has a negligible effect
on job satisfaction once individual-level characteristics are accounted for,
meaning the drivers of developer satisfaction appear to be largely universal
rather than country-specific. The proportional odds and linearity assumptions
were both supported empirically, lending credibility to the cumulative logit
framework used throughout.
